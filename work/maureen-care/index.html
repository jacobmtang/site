The problem

Binge eating disorder (BED) is the most common eating disorder in the U.S., affecting about 1.2% of American adults in a given year.1

Yet the coping strategies people are given are hard to use in the moment. Therapeutic advice usually comes as static paper worksheets, or as general guidance from a dietitian or therapist: follow a breathing pattern or slow down your eating. When an urge hits, there's nothing dynamic or all-in-one to guide them through it. The tools are disconnected, and rarely within reach when they're needed.

1 National Institute of Mental Health, “Eating Disorders”, from the National Comorbidity Survey Replication (Hudson et al., 2007).

Who it's for

Maureen is built for users with two overlapping needs.

Therapeutic support, in the moment. People who can't afford therapy, or need help between sessions, get research-validated coping techniques from Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) and Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) right when they need them.

Dietary structure, without the numbers. People who need structure around food, but for whom calorie counting and macro calculations are inappropriate, as they invite hypervigilance and restrictive behaviors.

What shipped
